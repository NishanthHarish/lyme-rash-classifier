Why this matters

Lyme disease is easy to treat when it is caught early, and easy to miss.

1

A tiny tick bite

Lyme disease is an infection passed on by a tick bite, often picked up in grass, woods or gardens. Many people never notice the bite.

2

A slowly growing red rash

A few days to a month later, a red rash often spreads out from the bite. It can look like a bullseye, but is often just an even red patch.

3

Early treatment works

Caught early, it is usually cured with a short course of antibiotics. Missed, it can lead to joint pain, nerve problems or heart trouble later.

4

But rashes look alike

Insect bites, ringworm, hives and skin infections can look similar, so people often wait or are unsure whether to see a doctor.

This project helps with that first question, "Could this rash be Lyme disease?", by giving a quick second opinion from a computer program trained on hundreds of rash photos. It is meant to get people to a doctor sooner, not to replace one.

Reading your result

  • Looks like a Lyme rash. The photo resembles Lyme rashes the program has seen. Please show it to a doctor soon and mention any tick bite or time outdoors.
  • Inconclusive. The program can't tell either way. Treat it as a reason to get the rash checked.
  • Does not look like a Lyme rash. It looks more like another skin problem. This is not an all-clear: if the rash keeps growing, or you feel feverish, tired or achy, see a doctor.
  • Not a rash photo. The program couldn't find a skin rash in the picture. Try again with a closer, well-lit photo.

In testing, the program was right about 3 times out of 4. It spotted roughly 8 out of 10 Lyme rashes, and about 1 in 4 other rashes were wrongly flagged as possible Lyme. That balance is deliberate: missing a Lyme rash is worse than a false alarm.

Under the hood: model and test results

Image features come from two networks pretrained on ImageNet, EfficientNetV2-S and ResNet-50. Two small classifiers sit on top. The first decides whether the photo shows a skin rash at all; it was trained on about 4,100 extra photos of everyday objects, scenes and healthy skin. The second scores how much the rash resembles erythema migrans rather than 12 look-alike conditions such as ringworm, cellulitis, hives and spider bites. The cut-off was chosen by cross-validation, and scores close to it are reported as inconclusive.

Measured on held-out photos not used for training. Near-duplicate photos were grouped before splitting, so no copy of a test photo was seen in training.

Limitations. About 970 unique web images of rashes were available, and clinical photos may look different. There are no skin-tone, body-site or lesion-stage labels, so performance across those groups is unknown; erythema migrans is harder to see on darker skin.

Not a medical device. This is a research project and has not been clinically validated. It cannot diagnose or rule out Lyme disease. If you have had a tick bite or have an expanding rash, fever or joint pain, see a doctor.
