How it works

One neural network answers two questions: is this a photo of a skin rash at all, and if so, does it look like a Lyme rash?

1

Scope check

The model was also trained on thousands of everyday photos and healthy skin (hands, arms, legs, faces). If the photo looks like one of those, it says so instead of guessing.

2

Rash classification

For rash photos, features from two pretrained networks (EfficientNetV2-S and ResNet-50) feed a classifier trained on erythema migrans and 12 look-alike conditions (ringworm, cellulitis, hives, spider bites and more) that scores how Lyme-like the rash is.

3

Calibrated decision

The cut-off was chosen by cross-validation to balance catching Lyme rashes against false alarms. Scores close to the cut-off are reported as inconclusive.

Performance

Measured on held-out test images the model never saw. Near-duplicate photos were grouped before splitting, so no copy of a test image was used in training.

Limitations

  • Trained on about 970 unique web images of rashes; real clinical photos may look different.
  • The dataset has no skin-tone, body-site or lesion-stage labels, so performance across those groups is unknown. Erythema migrans is harder to see on darker skin.
  • Some rashes are mimics of each other even for dermatologists; a photo alone cannot confirm Lyme disease.
Not a medical device. This is a research and portfolio project and has not been clinically validated. It cannot diagnose or rule out Lyme disease. If you have had a tick bite or have an expanding rash, fever or joint pain, see a doctor.
